## 1. The problem-space hypothesis (Newell and Simon 1972; 1976 Turing lecture)

### Takeaway
Newell and Simon treated problem solving as search through a problem space: a set of states (knowledge states), operators that move between them, an initial state and goal states. Heuristics guide the search because only part of the space can be explored. Neither primary text could be opened here. The opened sources only confirm the framing secondhand.

### Cited Findings
- Newell, Shaw and Simon "characterized problem solving as search through a combinatorial problem space, represented as a tree". The Tree of Thoughts paper takes this as the inspiration for search over intermediate "thoughts". It cites Newell, Shaw and Simon (1959, "Report on a general problem solving program") and Newell and Simon (1972, *Human Problem Solving*). — [Yao et al., Tree of Thoughts, arXiv 2305.10601](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.10601)
- Tree of Thoughts quotes Newell et al. as its epigraph: "A genuine problem-solving process involves the repeated use of available information to initiate exploration, which discloses, in turn, more information until a way to attain the solution is finally discovered." — [arXiv 2305.10601](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.10601)
- The Logic Theorist (Newell, Shaw and Simon, 1956) used heuristic search strategies and was first offered as a model of human problem solving. It was then revised many times, and the line of work led to means-ends analysis and the General Problem Solver. — [Moews and Shrager, "Executable Archaeology: Reanimating the Logic Theorist", arXiv 2611.19167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.19167)
- [search snippet only] One arXiv paper restates Problem Space Theory as a triple (initial state, operators, goal state): solving a problem means finding an operator path between them, and limited cognitive resources allow only partial unfolding of the space, which is why heuristics are needed. — via search on [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.06948v1) (not read in full)
- [NOT OPENED: standard reference] Newell, A. and Simon, H. A. (1972). *Human Problem Solving*. Prentice-Hall. The book states the "problem space" hypothesis: problem solving takes place in a problem space of knowledge states, with operators, an initial state and a goal test.
- [NOT OPENED: standard reference] Newell, A. and Simon, H. A. (1976). "Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search." *Communications of the ACM* 19(3):113–126, doi:10.1145/360018.360022. The lecture states the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis ("a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action") and the Heuristic Search Hypothesis (symbol systems solve problems by generating potential solutions and progressively modifying them, i.e. by search). These quotations are from memory and were not verified against the paper; dl.acm.org was blocked.

### Inferences
- For a benchmark-design problem, the problem-space view tells the student to state four things explicitly: (a) what a "state" is (e.g. a partial or complete assignment of factor levels), (b) the operators (set or change one factor, add or remove a run), (c) the initial state, and (d) the goal test or evaluation criterion. The choice of representation decides which search methods apply.

## 2. Standard search-problem formulation (AIMA); uninformed vs informed search; admissible/consistent heuristics and A* optimality; local search

### Takeaway
A search problem is a tuple (state space, initial state, actions, transition model, goal test, action/path cost). A* orders nodes by f = g + h. If h is admissible (never overestimates the cost to go), A* with node reopening returns an optimal solution. If h is also consistent (h(x) ≤ c(x,x') + h(x')), no reopening is needed. Local search (hill climbing, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms) works on complete states and offers no optimality guarantee in general.

### Cited Findings
- AIMA pseudocode treats a problem as an object with an INITIAL-STATE and successors. HILL-CLIMBING "returns a state that is a local maximum": it repeatedly moves to "a highest-valued successor" and stops when no neighbor improves on VALUE(current). The figure caption calls it "the most basic local search technique. At each step the current node is replaced by the best neighbor." — [AIMA pseudocode, Hill-Climbing (AIMA4e, Fig. 4.2)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aimacode/aima-pseudocode/master/md/Hill-Climbing.md)
- SIMULATED-ANNEALING(problem, schedule) picks a random successor and computes ΔE = VALUE(next) − VALUE(current). It always accepts if ΔE > 0, accepts otherwise "only with probability e^(ΔE/T)", and returns when the schedule's temperature T reaches 0. The caption says: "higher temperatures early in the schedule mean that downhill moves are accepted more readily; late in the schedule with low temperatures, downhill moves are mostly rejected." — [AIMA pseudocode, Simulated-Annealing (AIMA4e, Fig. 4.6)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aimacode/aima-pseudocode/master/md/Simulated-Annealing.md)
- GENETIC-ALGORITHM(population, FITNESS-FN) repeats population ← [MUTATE(RECOMBINE(SELECT(2, population, FITNESS-FN)))] "until some individual is fit enough, or enough time has elapsed". SELECT is tournament-like: it takes a uniform sample of 2ρ individuals and keeps the top ρ by fitness. RECOMBINE is single-point crossover. — [AIMA pseudocode, Genetic-Algorithm (AIMA4e)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aimacode/aima-pseudocode/master/md/Genetic-Algorithm.md)
- The A* algorithm as stated (Pearl 1984 style): expand the Open node with the smallest f(x) = g(x) + h(x). For each successor x', if g(x') > g(x) + c(x,x'), "direct the pointer of x' back to x and reopen x' if it is in Closed". — [Inconsistency and Accuracy of Heuristics with A* Search, arXiv 1307.2200](https://arxiv.org/html/1307.2200)
- "An important property of A* search is its admissibility: A* will always return an optimal solution if the heuristic h it uses is admissible, meaning h(x) never exceeds h*(x)." — [arXiv 1307.2200](https://arxiv.org/html/1307.2200)
- Consistency: "Heuristic h is called consistent if h(x) ≤ c*(x,x') + h(x') for all pairs of nodes". This is equivalent to monotonicity, h(x) ≤ c(x,x') + h(x') for every edge (Pearl 1984, Thm. 8). Consistency was introduced in the original A* paper (Hart, Nilsson and Raphael 1968) and "is believed to enable A* to forgo reopening nodes". — [arXiv 1307.2200](https://arxiv.org/html/1307.2200)
- Dominance by informedness (Pearl 1984, Thm. 7): if h1 and h2 are both admissible and h1(x) > h2(x) for every non-solution node, then every node expanded by A*(h1) is also expanded by A*(h2). — [arXiv 1307.2200](https://arxiv.org/html/1307.2200)
- In A*, "nodes are selected according to f̂ = g + h, where g is the cost accumulated so far and h is an admissible lower bound on the remaining cost (Hart et al., 1968). As originally defined, A* performs graph search: its Open and Closed structures detect when a state is regenerated along a different path, and only the cheapest path to each state is retained." — [Cohen, Kress, Nehrke, Pesch and Shtern, "Two Communities, One Problem: Branch-and-Bound vs. A* for RCPSP", arXiv 2611.14426 (Nov 2026)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14426v1)
- Worked state-space formulation (RCPSP): a state records the system time g(s), the completed activities and the ongoing activities with their residual durations. The initial state s0 has nothing completed and g = 0. The goal states S_f are all states in which every activity is complete. Transitions are scheduling decisions, the reachable states form a "state graph" G = (S, E), and "finding an optimal schedule is equivalent to finding a path from the initial state to a goal state that minimizes the system time of the goal state." — [arXiv 2611.14426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14426v1)
- [search snippet only] If the heuristic is consistent, A* without reopening returns an optimal solution. With an admissible but inconsistent heuristic, nodes can be reopened, and reopening can cost Ω(2^n) expansions in the worst case. — snippets from [arXiv 2509.22626](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.22626) and [arXiv 2504.21131](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.21131) (not read in full)
- [NOT OPENED: standard reference] Russell, S. and Norvig, P., *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, 4th ed. (2020), ch. 3–4 and ch. 6. AIMA formalizes a problem as: state space, initial state, ACTIONS(s), transition model RESULT(s,a), goal test IS-GOAL(s), and action cost c(s,a,s'), with path cost as the sum of action costs. Uninformed strategies (BFS, uniform-cost, DFS, depth-limited, iterative deepening, bidirectional) are compared on completeness, cost-optimality, time and space. AIMA's stated results: A* tree search is cost-optimal with an admissible h, and A* graph search (no reopening) is cost-optimal with a consistent h. Every consistent h is admissible. These are standard textbook results; the AIMA text itself was not opened (aima.cs.berkeley.edu was blocked).

### Inferences
- When the "path" does not matter and only the final design does, as in benchmark design, the problem is an optimization over complete states. AIMA's ch. 4 local search (hill climbing, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms) and branch and bound (section 3) then fit better than path-finding A*. A* applies if the design is built incrementally (assign one factor per step) with an additive cost and an admissible bound on the cost-to-go.
- Hill climbing returns a local maximum by construction, per its pseudocode. Simulated annealing and genetic algorithms terminate by schedule or budget ("enough time has elapsed"). None of the three certifies global optimality. Only exhaustive or bounded methods (A* with an admissible h, branch and bound with valid bounds) give exactness guarantees.

## 3. Branch and bound: bounding, exact pruning, node selection; multi-objective B&B and bound sets

### Takeaway
Branch and bound (Land and Doig 1960) enumerates implicitly. It partitions the feasible set into subproblems and discards a subproblem when a valid bound proves it cannot beat the incumbent. Pruning is exact only if the bound really is a bound: a lower bound for minimization, typically from a relaxation. Best-first node selection minimizes the number of nodes but uses more memory. Depth-first finds feasible incumbents fast with little memory. With several objectives there is no single incumbent value, so each bound becomes a set (a lower bound set and an upper bound set, after Ehrgott and Gandibleux 2007). A node is fathomed only when its whole lower bound set is dominated, which makes bounding and branching considerably harder.

### Cited Findings
- B&B "was initially proposed by Land and Doig to solve discrete programming problems. This method implicitly enumerates all possible solutions by iteratively dividing the original problem into a series of sub-problems, organized in a tree structure, and discarding the sub-problems where a global optimum cannot be found… Once the entire tree has been explored, the exact solution can be achieved." — [Branch and Bound in MILP: A Survey of Techniques and Trends, arXiv 2111.06257](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.06257v1)
- The standard pruning rules for minimization: prune by bound, when the node's lower bound exceeds the incumbent value (ž > ẑ); prune by infeasibility, when the relaxation is infeasible (lower bound = ∞); prune by integrality, when the relaxation optimum is feasible for the original problem. Nodes that meet any of these are "fathomed". Dominance relations (Kohler and Steiglitz) also allow pruning: if node S1 dominates S2, every descendant of S2 has a no-worse completion under S1. — [arXiv 2111.06257](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.06257v1)
- Node selection: depth-first (Dakin) "is the preferred strategy for pure feasibility problems… it has small memory consumption. However, depth-first search completely disregards the second goal to improve the global dual bound." Best-first "selects a leaf with the currently smallest dual objective value". Achterberg showed "there exists a node selection strategy of the best-first search type, which solves the instance in a minimal number of nodes." — [arXiv 2111.06257](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.06257v1)
- In ordinary B&B a lower bound, "often but not necessarily obtained from a relaxation, bounds the best schedule attainable within a node, while the best complete schedule found so far provides an upper bound. A node can be discarded if it is infeasible or if its lower bound is not smaller than the upper bound. Node selection is flexible and may follow depth-first, best-first, breadth-first, or hybrid strategies." — [arXiv 2611.14426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14426v1)
- A* and B&B are related. Nau, Kumar and Kanal (1984) defined General Branch and Bound (GBB) through representation, goal, selection, splitting and pruning functions, "general enough for both ordinary B&B and A* to be represented as special cases". In practice, A* merges duplicate states reached by different paths (graph search), while ordinary B&B keeps them as distinct nodes unless a dominance rule detects the equivalence. — [arXiv 2611.14426](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14426v1)
- Multi-objective definitions (minimization). y¹ dominates y² if y¹ ≤ y² componentwise and y¹ ≠ y². The non-dominated set is Y_N. Ehrgott and Gandibleux (2007) defined a lower bound set L for S_N (S_N ⊂ L + ℝ^p_≥, with L = L_N) and an upper bound set U (S_N ⊂ cl[ℝ^p \ (U + ℝ^p_≥)]). The ideal point is a trivial lower bound set and the nadir point a trivial upper bound set. Solving the LP relaxation of a multi-objective integer program yields a valid lower bound set. — [Forget, Gadegaard, Klamroth, Nielsen and Przybylski, "Enhancing Branch-and-Bound for Multi-Objective 0-1 Programming", arXiv 2210.05385](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.05385)
- Fathoming in multi-objective B&B happens in three ways. (i) By infeasibility. (ii) By optimality, when L(η) is a single extreme point with an integer pre-image. (iii) "By dominance… when the lower bound set L(η) is dominated by the upper bound set U… each feasible point of P^LP(η), and thus of P(η), is dominated by at least one already known integer point". In practice the node is fathomed if no local upper bound u ∈ N(U) lies in L(η) + ℝ^p_≥ (Sourd and Spanjaard 2008). The upper bound set starts empty and is updated with every non-dominated integer point found. — [arXiv 2210.05385](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.05385)
- Why it is harder: with three or more objectives, "generating sub-problems without redundancies is a much more complex task compared to the bi-objective case". Bound sets "are more complex to compute", and objective branching (splitting the objective space where L is partially dominated by U) "cannot be applied as often and easily as in the bi-objective case". — [arXiv 2210.05385](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.05385)
- In bi-objective B&B, "nodes can be pruned if every point in the lower bound set is dominated by a point in the upper bound set", and "partial pruning" discards dominated portions of a node. The approach uses bound sets as the lower bound, and in the authors' application those sets are computed by column generation. — [Parragh and Tricoire, "Branch-and-bound for bi-objective integer programming", arXiv 1809.06823](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06823)
- A self-contained survey of multi-objective B&B is Przybylski and Gandibleux (2017, EJOR). [search snippet only; survey NOT OPENED] — via [arXiv 2301.11974](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.11974)
- [NOT OPENED: standard reference] Land, A. H. and Doig, A. G. (1960). "An automatic method of solving discrete programming problems." *Econometrica* 28(3):497–520. Ehrgott, M. and Gandibleux, X. (2007). "Bound sets for biobjective combinatorial optimization problems." *Computers & Operations Research* 34(9):2674–2694.

### Inferences
- Exactness condition: B&B returns a certified optimum only if (a) branching partitions the space (every feasible design lies in some leaf), (b) the node bound is valid (it never overestimates the best attainable value in a minimization), and (c) pruning compares against a genuinely feasible incumbent. A heuristic "bound" that is sometimes optimistic in the wrong direction makes the pruning unsound. This is the same condition as admissibility in A*.
- If "best benchmark design" is judged on several criteria (e.g. cost, discriminative power, coverage), the target is the Pareto set, not one point. The student must use dominance-based fathoming with bound sets, or scalarize and accept that a weighted sum only recovers supported non-dominated points. The second part is my inference from Property 1 in the Forget et al. paper, which says weighted-sum optima of the LP relaxation lie at extreme points.

- Land and Doig (1960) and Ehrgott and Gandibleux (2007) were not opened. Their content is described only through the secondary surveys above.
- The claim that best-first is node-minimal relies on Achterberg's thesis as cited by the MILP survey; it was not checked in the original.

## 4. Constraint satisfaction as a feasibility representation

### Takeaway
A CSP is (X, D, C): variables, their domains, and constraints over subsets of variables. Arc consistency (AC-3) deletes domain values that have no supporting value in a neighbor. It either proves infeasibility (an empty domain) or shrinks the space before backtracking search. This is the natural way to encode feasibility rules for a constrained factorial design space, such as "factor A = x forbids factor B = y".

### Cited Findings
- AC-3 takes "a binary CSP with components (X, D, C)". It processes a queue of arcs (Xi, Xj). REVISE deletes x from Di "if no value y in Dj allows (x, y) to satisfy the constraint between Xi and Xj", and when Di shrinks, the arcs (Xk, Xi) for its neighbors are re-queued. "After applying AC-3, either every arc is arc-consistent, or some variable has an empty domain, indicating that the CSP cannot be solved." (Mackworth 1977) — [AIMA pseudocode, AC-3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aimacode/aima-pseudocode/master/md/AC-3.md)
- BACKTRACKING-SEARCH assigns one variable at a time (SELECT-UNASSIGNED-VARIABLE, ORDER-DOMAIN-VALUES), keeps only values consistent with the current assignment, and calls INFERENCE, which "can optionally be used to impose arc-, path-, or k-consistency". On failure it undoes the assignment and its inferences. The caption notes the algorithm "is modeled on the recursive depth-first search". — [AIMA pseudocode, Backtracking-Search](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aimacode/aima-pseudocode/master/md/Backtracking-Search.md)

### Inferences
- Arc consistency is sound: it never removes a value that belongs to some solution. It is incomplete in general, because arc-consistent CSPs can still be unsatisfiable. So propagation can prune infeasible factor combinations exactly, but finding feasible designs still needs search. Soundness follows from REVISE's definition. Incompleteness is standard CSP theory and was not verified in an opened source.
- A practical pipeline: encode the feasibility rules as a CSP, enumerate or sample feasible level combinations (the candidate set), then pick runs with a design criterion (section 6), e.g. D-optimal selection from the candidate set. This is how constrained-region designs are usually built; see Gaps.

- No opened source covered constraint optimization (weighted or soft constraints, COP) or the formal soundness and incompleteness statements for arc consistency.
- The NIST handbook pages on computer-aided designs for constrained regions were blocked.

## 5. Design of experiments: factorial and fractional designs, resolution, aliasing, orthogonal arrays/Taguchi, sparsity/hierarchy/heredity, screening (PB, DSD), RSM, mixed levels

### Takeaway
A 2^(d−q) regular fraction is defined by q generator words. Their 2^q − 1 products form the defining relation, and each effect is aliased with its products by the defining words. Resolution is the length of the shortest defining word: III aliases main effects with two-factor interactions (2FIs), IV keeps main effects clear of 2FIs but aliases 2FIs with each other, and V keeps both clear. A resolution-R design is an orthogonal array of strength ⌊R⌋ − 1. Non-regular orthogonal arrays such as Plackett–Burman designs allow run sizes that are multiples of 4 but have partial aliasing. Definitive screening designs use 2d + 1 three-level runs and keep main effects orthogonal to 2FIs. All small screening designs rely on effect sparsity, effect hierarchy and (for main-effects screening) strong effect heredity, and these principles can fail.

### Cited Findings
- Regular fractions: "The most widely used two-level fractional factorial designs are 1/2^q fractions of the 2^d full factorial design, known as 2^(d−q) designs… Two factorial effects that occur in the same linear combination cannot be independently estimated and are said to be aliased." — [Woods and Lewis, "Design of Experiments for Screening", arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Defining relation: choose defining words v1,…,vq. The corresponding effects are constant in the design (aliased with the mean), so 2^q − 1 effects are aliased with the mean, written I = v1 = … = vq = v1v2 = … = v1⋯vq. An effect with word vj is aliased with vj multiplied by every word in the defining relation. Example: I = x1x2x3x4 gives E(β̂1) = β1 + β234 and E(β̂12) = β12 + β34. — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Resolution: "A resolution III design has at least one main effect aliased with a two-variable interaction. A resolution IV design has no main effects aliased with interactions but at least one pair of two-variable interactions aliased together. A resolution V design has no main effects or two-variable interactions aliased with any other main effects or two-variable interactions. A more detailed… classification… is obtained via the aberration criterion." Resolution V designs "typically require too many runs for screening". — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Box and Hunter (1961) define resolution R as: no c-factor effect is aliased with any effect containing fewer than R − c factors. "In two-level regular fractional factorials, its resolution is the length of the shortest word in the defining relation and is one more than the strength of the corresponding orthogonal array." Deng and Tang (1999) generalized resolution to non-regular designs: R(D) = r + [1 − max J_r(S)/N]. "A regular or nonregular fractional factorial of resolution R is an orthogonal array of strength ⌊R⌋ − 1." Minimum aberration is from Fries and Hunter (1980). — [Orthogonal Arrays: A Review, arXiv 2505.15032](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15032v1)
- Orthogonal array definition: an N × k array with s levels has strength t and index λ "if every N × t subarray… contains each t-tuple… exactly λ times as a row". A mixed (asymmetrical) OA(N, s1^k1 s2^k2 ⋯ sv^kv, t) allows factors with different numbers of levels. Example: OA(12, 2^4 3^1, 2), with four 2-level factors and one 3-level factor in 12 runs. — [arXiv 2505.15032](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15032v1)
- Projection property: "if there are at most t active… factors, then an orthogonal array of strength t allows for the estimation of all factorial effects of these active factors… regardless of which factors are active." Also, a strength-t OA "is universally optimal for any model consisting of factorial effects involving at most ⌊t/2⌋ factors (Cheng, 1980)." — [arXiv 2505.15032](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15032v1)
- Rao's bound gives a lower bound on N for an OA(N, s^k, t). The linear-programming bound (Delsarte 1973) is always at least as strong. — [arXiv 2505.15032](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15032v1)
- Plackett–Burman designs are orthogonal arrays OA(n, 2^(n−1), 2) built from Hadamard matrices (CᵀC = nIₙ), with n a multiple of 4 (12, 20, 24, …). They estimate all d = n − 1 main effects independently "assuming negligible interactions". The cost is partial aliasing: in the 12-run PB design, "the main effect of each variable is partially aliased with all 45 interactions that do not include that variable". By contrast, the 16-run resolution III 2^(11−7) fraction aliases each main effect with at most four 2FIs. Partial aliasing does, however, let interactions be found later by variable-selection methods. — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Definitive screening designs (DSDs; Jones and Nachtsheim 2011): three-level designs for d factors in "as few as n = 2d + 1 runs". They have one centre point and d mirrored (foldover) pairs. In the jth pair the jth factor is 0 and the others are ±1. The foldover "ensures that main effects and two-variable interactions are orthogonal… unlike for resolution III or PB designs". Quadratic effects are estimated independently of main effects but not of 2FIs, and the 2FIs are partially aliased with each other. — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Supersaturated designs (n < d + 1) are workable only if "sparsity holds and the active main effects are large". Construction criteria include E(s²)-optimality and Bayesian D-optimality. — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Effect (factor) sparsity: "only a small proportion of the input variables have a substantive influence on the output. If this widely-observed principle does not hold, then a small screening experiment may fail to reliably detect the active variables." — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Effect hierarchy: "low-order factorial effects, such as main effects and two-variable interactions, are more likely to be important than higher-order effects. This principle is supported by substantial empirical evidence from physical experiments." — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- Strong effect heredity: "important interactions or other nonlinearities involve only those input variables that have large main effects. Without this property, active variables may be overlooked… There is evidence, particularly from industrial experiments, that strong effect heredity may fail to hold in practice." — [arXiv 1510.05248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.05248)
- [search snippet only] Weak heredity requires only that at least one parent main effect of an active interaction is active. — via search results on [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.09225) (not read)
- [NOT OPENED: standard references] Taguchi designs and robust parameter design: Taguchi, G. (1987), *System of Experimental Design*. Taguchi's "L-arrays" (L8, L9, L12, L18, …) are orthogonal arrays used with inner (control) and outer (noise) arrays and signal-to-noise ratios. Response surface methodology: Box, G. E. P. and Wilson, K. B. (1951), *JRSS-B* 13:1–45. It covers sequential first-order steepest ascent, followed by second-order designs (central composite, Box–Behnken) to fit and optimize a quadratic surface. None of these were opened. The NIST e-Handbook sections that cover them (pri3, pri5) were blocked.

### Inferences
- For a constrained mixed-level benchmark space (e.g. some factors at 2 levels, some at 3 or more, and infeasible combinations), regular 2^(d−q) fractions usually do not apply directly. The options are mixed-level orthogonal arrays where they exist and the constraints allow, or computer-generated (D-/A-optimal) designs over the feasible candidate set.
- The design's resolution or strength should match the model the student intends to fit. Assuming "only main effects matter" means relying on hierarchy and strong heredity, which Woods and Lewis note can fail. If interactions between benchmark factors are plausible (e.g. task type × prompt format), use resolution IV/V, strength ≥ 3, or a DSD-like foldover structure.

## 6. Optimal and Bayesian experimental design: D-/A-optimality, EIG, BOED, adaptive/sequential design

### Takeaway
Classical optimal design ranks designs by a scalar summary of the (Fisher) information matrix. D-optimality maximizes |XᵀX|, which minimizes the volume of the confidence ellipsoid. A-optimality minimizes tr[(XᵀX)⁻¹], the sum of parameter variances. For non-linear models these criteria depend on the unknown parameters. Bayesian experimental design instead maximizes the expected information gain, EIG(ξ) = mutual information between parameters and outcome. EIG is costly to estimate (nested Monte Carlo), and sequential greedy EIG maximization is myopic.

### Cited Findings
- "The D-criterion ranks designs according to |(LᵀL)⁻¹|… smaller values are desirable", or equivalently maximizes |LᵀL|. Under normal errors, "the D-optimal and Ds-optimal designs minimize the volume of the confidence ellipsoids". These criteria suit "an overall test of the significance of all effects, but not necessarily… individual testing". "The A-criterion ranks designs with respect to tr[{LᵀL}⁻¹]… the sum of the individual variances of the parameters of interest." — [Stallrich, Allen-Moyer and Jones, "D- and A-Optimal Screening Designs", arXiv 2210.13943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13943)
- Caveat on D-optimality for screening: for main-effect models a D-optimal design with all xij = ±1 always exists (Box and Draper 1971). However, "infinitely many D-optimal designs" can exist with different individual variances, so the authors argue the A-criterion separates screening designs better. — [arXiv 2210.13943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13943)
- The determinant and trace "correspond to the criteria of D- and A-optimality, respectively, in optimal design of experiments." — [arXiv 2505.15032](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15032v1)
- BED setup: a prior p(θ) and a simulator or likelihood p(y|θ,ξ). The information gain is InfoGain_θ(ξ,y) = H[p(θ)] − H[p(θ|y,ξ)]. The design criterion is EIG_θ(ξ) = E_{p(θ)p(y|θ,ξ)}[log p(y|θ,ξ) − log p(y|ξ)], which equals "the mutual information between y and θ given ξ… or the expected KL divergence from the posterior to the prior". More generally, BED optimizes E[U(ξ,θ,y)] for a utility U. — [Rainforth, Foster, Ivanova and Bickford Smith, "Modern Bayesian Experimental Design", arXiv 2302.14545 (Statistical Science)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14545)
- Classical (frequentist) optimal design maximizes a functional of the Fisher information matrix, which gives "the classical 'alphabetic criteria'". Drawbacks: (1) these summaries "can vary with the precise parameterization of the model". (2) "The FIM is a function of the unknown 'true' parameters, θ". It is independent of θ only for linear-Gaussian models, so otherwise one must approximate the model, plug in a point estimate (locally optimal design), or average over a prior. — [arXiv 2302.14545](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14545)
- Computational cost: estimating EIG "requires an additional nested mechanism for estimating p(y_n|ξ)", traditionally done with nested Monte Carlo or nested Laplace approximations. Gradient-based EIG optimization struggles "when the design space or likelihood function is not continuous, for example because some design decisions are discrete". — [arXiv 2302.14545](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14545)
- Adaptive design (BAD): for steps t = 1…T with history h_{t−1}, choose ξ_t to maximize the incremental EIG_θ(ξ_t | h_{t−1}), run the experiment, update the posterior, and repeat. This is "usually more effective than static design approaches". Updating all model parameters ensures "the sum of the incremental EIGs is equal to the total EIG". — [arXiv 2302.14545](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14545)
- The traditional BAD approach "is actually subtly sub-optimal because it makes greedy, myopic, decisions: it fails to account for the fact that different design choices at one step can also influence the information gained from future steps". Deep Adaptive Design learns non-myopic policies up front. BAD can be formulated as a Bayes-adaptive MDP with incremental EIG as reward. Bayesian active learning and Bayesian optimization are named as special cases. — [arXiv 2302.14545](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14545)

### Inferences
- For "best benchmark design", D-/A-optimality is appropriate when the goal is precise estimation of a pre-specified linear model of factor effects (main effects plus chosen interactions) over a constrained candidate set. The criterion is then parameter-free, because the linear-Gaussian FIM does not depend on θ. If the analysis model is non-linear (e.g. logistic accuracy models or IRT), the design depends on θ. A Bayesian (EIG) or prior-averaged criterion is then the principled choice, at higher computational cost, and greedy sequential EIG is not globally optimal.
- Choosing which runs to include is itself a combinatorial search over subsets of the candidate set. Exchange algorithms (such as the coordinate exchange referenced by Stallrich et al.) are local search in the sense of section 2. They do not certify global optimality unless paired with an exact bound-based method.
